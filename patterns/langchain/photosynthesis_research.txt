Photosynthetic Efficiency in C3 and C4 Plants Under Elevated CO2 Conditions

Abstract

This study examines the comparative photosynthetic efficiency of C3 and C4 plant species under varying atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations. Field trials were conducted across three growing seasons to measure carbon fixation rates, water use efficiency, and biomass accumulation. Results indicate that C4 plants maintain superior photosynthetic performance under standard atmospheric conditions, while C3 plants show disproportionate gains when CO2 levels are elevated above 600 ppm. These findings have implications for crop selection strategies in a changing climate.

Introduction

Photosynthesis is the biochemical process by which plants convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose molecules. Two primary photosynthetic pathways have evolved in terrestrial plants: the C3 pathway, used by approximately 85 percent of plant species including wheat, rice, and most trees, and the C4 pathway, used by roughly 3 percent of species including maize, sugarcane, and sorghum. The C4 pathway evolved independently multiple times as an adaptation to hot, dry, and low-CO2 environments, allowing these plants to minimize water loss while maintaining efficient carbon fixation.

The distinction between these pathways centers on how each plant captures and processes carbon dioxide. C3 plants fix CO2 directly into a three-carbon compound through the enzyme RuBisCO. C4 plants first fix CO2 into a four-carbon compound in mesophyll cells before shuttling it to specialized bundle sheath cells, where RuBisCO operates in a CO2-rich environment, significantly reducing a wasteful process called photorespiration.

Methodology

Field trials were established at two research sites with contrasting climates: a temperate site in central Iowa and a semi-arid site in eastern Colorado. At each site, twelve plant species were grown across four CO2 treatment chambers maintaining concentrations of 400 ppm (ambient baseline), 550 ppm, 700 ppm, and 850 ppm. Each chamber housed replicate plots of six C3 species (wheat, rice, soybean, oat, barley, and Arabidopsis) and six C4 species (maize, sugarcane, sorghum, millet, switchgrass, and big bluestem).

Photosynthetic rates were measured using infrared gas analyzers attached to leaf chambers, sampling carbon assimilation every four hours during daylight periods across the full 120-day growing season. Water use efficiency was calculated as the ratio of carbon dioxide assimilated to water transpired, measured continuously using sap flow sensors. Biomass was harvested and dried at the end of each season, then weighed to determine total accumulation per treatment group.

Statistical analysis used a mixed-effects model accounting for site, species, CO2 treatment, and their interactions, with growing season included as a random effect to account for year-to-year climate variation.

Results

Under ambient CO2 conditions of 400 ppm, C4 species demonstrated photosynthetic rates approximately 35 percent higher than C3 species when normalized for leaf area, consistent with the established understanding that C4 plants outperform C3 plants in current atmospheric conditions. This advantage was most pronounced in the semi-arid Colorado site, where C4 plants also showed 28 percent better water use efficiency.

As CO2 concentration increased, however, the gap narrowed considerably. At 700 ppm, C3 species showed a 42 percent increase in photosynthetic rate compared to their ambient baseline, while C4 species showed only an 8 percent increase. By 850 ppm, C3 wheat and rice varieties had closed nearly the entire performance gap with C4 maize and sorghum in terms of raw carbon fixation, though C4 species retained their water use efficiency advantage across all CO2 treatments.

Biomass accumulation followed a similar pattern. C3 species at 850 ppm produced 31 percent more dry biomass than their 400 ppm counterparts, while C4 species at the same elevated concentration showed only a 6 percent biomass increase relative to ambient conditions. This is explained by the fact that C4 plants already operate near CO2 saturation for their RuBisCO enzyme due to their internal concentrating mechanism, leaving little room for additional gains when atmospheric CO2 rises.

Soybean, a C3 legume, showed the single largest response to elevated CO2 among all twelve species tested, with a 51 percent increase in photosynthetic rate at 850 ppm compared to ambient conditions. Switchgrass, a C4 species, showed the smallest response at just 3 percent.

Discussion

These results carry significant implications for agricultural planning under projected climate scenarios. Current atmospheric CO2 concentration stands at approximately 420 ppm and is projected to reach 550 to 600 ppm by mid-century under moderate emissions scenarios. Our findings suggest that C3 crops such as wheat, rice, and soybean may see meaningful yield benefits from rising CO2 levels, partially offsetting yield losses expected from associated temperature increases and water stress.

However, this CO2 fertilization effect should not be interpreted as a net positive for agriculture. Elevated CO2 has been independently associated with reduced nutritional density in C3 crops, particularly reductions in protein, zinc, and iron content, a phenomenon documented in prior literature and not directly measured in this study. The carbon fixation gains observed here therefore may come at the cost of nutritional quality, a tradeoff that warrants further investigation.

C4 crops, while showing limited direct response to elevated CO2, remain critical for regions facing water scarcity due to their superior water use efficiency. Sorghum and millet in particular are likely to remain important staple crops in semi-arid regions of Africa and South Asia regardless of atmospheric CO2 trends, precisely because their advantage lies in drought resilience rather than carbon fixation ceiling.

Limitations

This study was conducted using controlled CO2 chambers that, while replicating field conditions closely, cannot fully capture the complexity of open-air ecosystems with natural pest pressure, soil microbiome interactions, and unrestricted root growth. Additionally, the three-season duration, while longer than many comparable studies, may not capture longer-term adaptive responses that could emerge over multiple plant generations or evolving soil conditions. Future research should extend observation periods and incorporate free-air CO2 enrichment methodology to validate these chamber-based findings under fully natural field conditions.

Conclusion

This research confirms that C3 and C4 photosynthetic pathways respond very differently to rising atmospheric CO2, with C3 plants showing substantial gains in carbon fixation and biomass while C4 plants remain comparatively unresponsive due to their already CO2-saturated internal biochemistry. Agricultural and policy planning for climate adaptation should account for this divergence, recognizing that C3 staple crops may partially benefit from rising CO2 even as C4 crops continue to offer unmatched water efficiency advantages in increasingly water-stressed growing regions.
